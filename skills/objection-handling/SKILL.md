# Handle the objection by surfacing it, not spinning it

> An objection is information, not rejection. The founder's instinct is to argue it away. Wrong move. The job is to find out what's actually true: is this a real blocker, or a polite way of saying no? You cannot fix a stall you won't name out loud.

**Use this when:** the founder keeps hitting the same objections and either folds ("okay, no worries") or over-argues (talks the prospect out of their own concern). Also good before a call, to pre-load responses.

## The core idea

Most objections are surface. "Let me think about it" is rarely about thinking. Underneath is one of three things: no real urgency, an unspoken concern, or the wrong person to decide. The QC move is always the same: surface the real objection honestly, then respond to *that*, not the surface line. If it turns out to be a genuine disqualifier, respect it and move on. Chasing a dead deal is more expensive than losing it.

Reads `docs/qc-sales/sales-brief.md` (the real competition, the buyer, the price) and `docs/qc-sales/coaching-log.md` (which objections keep recurring for this founder).

## How to run this (for the agent)

1. Read the brief and the log if they exist. Do not require them. If there is no `docs/qc-sales/sales-brief.md` yet (for example this skill is being run on its own, before `00-sales-setup`), do not stop or ask the founder to go create one. Just ask for the couple of things you need inline (product, who adopts vs who buys, and the deal in question) and continue. This pack works on its own.
2. Ask which objection they're facing and, if they have it, the exact words the prospect used. Words matter; coach to the real ones.
3. Give: what it usually really means, the surfacing move, the honest response, and the disqualifier test (when to respect it and walk).
4. Never hand the founder a manipulative script. The goal is truth, not a trick. A win built on spin closes and then churns.
5. If the objection is really about price, motion, or a competitive bake-off, flag it for the QC advisor (see escalation).

## The playbook

**"Let me think about it."**
- Usually means: no urgency, or an unspoken concern.
- Surface: "Totally fair. When people say that, it's usually one of two things: it's not a priority right now, or something about it doesn't quite fit. Which is closer?"
- Honest response: work whichever they name. Don't sell past it.
- Disqualifier test: if there's genuinely no priority and no trigger, it's a soft no. Mark it, set a real future check-in, stop spending hours.

**"No budget."**
- Usually means: the pain isn't big enough to fund, or you're talking to someone without budget authority.
- Surface: "Is it that the budget's committed elsewhere, or that this isn't yet worth carving one out for?"
- Honest response: if the pain is real, the next step is access to whoever owns the budget. If it isn't, go back to discovery, you haven't found a fundable problem.
- Disqualifier test: real pain plus no path to budget owner is a stall you can work. No pain is a no.

**"We'll just build it ourselves."**
- Usually means: they don't yet see the true cost of building and maintaining it, or your value isn't differentiated enough to beat "free."
- Surface: "A lot of teams start there. What's your honest read on what it costs to build and keep maintaining it versus the problem it's solving?"
- Honest response: make the maintenance and opportunity cost concrete. Don't trash their engineers. Frame it as focus, is this the thing they want their best people spending quarters on?
- Disqualifier test: if they have the team, the time, and it's core to their product, respect it. If it's a side problem, the build-it instinct usually fades once the cost is real.

**"We already use [incumbent]."**
- Usually means: not in enough pain to switch, or hasn't seen where you're genuinely better for their case.
- Surface: "Makes sense. Where does [incumbent] fall short for your specific setup, if anywhere?" Let them tell you the gap.
- Honest response: speak only to the gap they name. Never a feature-by-feature teardown; negative selling is believed but resented.
- Disqualifier test: if the incumbent works fine for them, it's a no today. Leave the door open for when the gap bites.

**"It's too early / we're not ready."**
- Usually means: real timing issue, or a soft no in disguise.
- Surface: "What would need to be true for this to be the right time?"
- Honest response: if they can name a concrete trigger and date, log it and follow up then. If they can't, it's a polite no.
- Disqualifier test: no nameable trigger equals no real intent.

**"I need to run it past the team / my boss."**
- Usually means: they're not the decision-maker, or they're deflecting.
- Surface: "Happy to. What do you think they'll want to know, and would it help if I joined that conversation?"
- Honest response: get into the room with the actual decision-maker. A champion selling internally on your behalf usually loses.
- Disqualifier test: if they won't give you access and won't advocate, the deal is stalled at the wrong level.

**"Send me some pricing / info."**
- Usually means: a polite exit, or a real need to evaluate cost.
- Surface: "Happy to. So I send the right thing, what's the main question you're trying to answer, budget, fit, or how it works?"
- Honest response: send one specific thing tied to their answer, with a next step attached. Never a brochure with no ask.
- Disqualifier test: if they won't say what they're evaluating and won't take a next step, it's a soft no.

**"We need a security / compliance review."**
- Usually means: often a real, genuine buying signal (they're serious enough to vet you), sometimes a slow-walk.
- Surface: "Great, that usually means it's getting real. Who runs that review and what do they need from me to start?"
- Honest response: this is where a QC advisor and real security docs matter. Get the process moving with a named owner and a date.
- Disqualifier test: a real review has a named owner and timeline. A vague one is a stall.

## Mistakes that look reasonable

- **Arguing the objection away.** You can win the argument and lose the deal. Surface, don't debate.
- **Folding instantly.** "No worries" on the first stall trains you to lose. One honest surfacing question is not pushy.
- **Discounting to kill an objection.** Price cuts answer a value problem you haven't diagnosed. They teach the buyer to wait.
- **Treating every objection as real.** Some are polite exits. Naming them lets you stop wasting time.
- **Treating every objection as fake.** Some are real disqualifiers. Respecting them is faster than chasing.

## Escalate to your QC advisor when

- The objection is really about **price or packaging**. That's a value and pricing conversation, not a rebuttal. Don't let the founder improvise a discount.
- It's a **competitive bake-off** against a funded incumbent. Positioning strategy is an advisor call.
- A **security or procurement** process has started on a meaningful deal. The advisor should help run it.
- The same objection is killing most deals. That's an ICP or positioning signal, not an objection-handling problem.
